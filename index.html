The Governing Question

When the subcontinent's schools were remade, who decided what they were for, in what language they taught, who certified what was learned, who might learn, and who paid, and what became of each answer?

The Atlas of Educational Philosophy asks what each tradition held education to be for. This atlas asks what became of those answers in one place. It follows the subcontinent from the Mughal grant to the Single National Curriculum through the documents that decided its schools: minutes, despatches, charters, commission reports, petitions, syllabi and the books that argued with them.

The question has six parts because a purpose does not reach a classroom on its own. It travels in a language. It is certified by someone. It is offered to some children and withheld from others. Someone pays for it. And it survives, is converted into something else, or is let go. A document can state a fine purpose and lose it at any of these points. Each entry therefore answers all six questions, and the Big Picture sets the answers side by side, period by period.

The quarrel of 1835 between the Orientalists and the Anglicists sits near the middle of the atlas, and it shows the pattern early. Both sides argued about language and books. Neither argued about ends, because both took the end for granted: the governance of India. The decisive change came twenty years later, when Wood's Despatch of 1854 and the universities of 1857 moved the warrant for learning from the teacher to an examining board. The board certified content. It had no place to put a purpose.

The subcontinent came out of colonial rule with institutions that certified without saying what they were for, and traditions that knew what they were for and had lost their institutions. After 1947 each state kept the machine and wrote its purposes into the preamble.

That is a claim, and the atlas is built to test it. The entries include the cases that cut against it: the purpose experiments that worked for a time, Dhaka's teaching university, Bangladesh's divergence after 1971 and Malaysia's written philosophy. Each entry also marks what still needs checking against the source.

1Purpose

What was it for?

Formation within a tradition, reform, service of the state, the nation, manpower, emancipation, the whole person, access, or measured learning?

2Medium

In what language?

A classical language, English, the learner's own tongue, a national language, or more than one?

3Warrant

Who certified the learning?

The teacher, a patron, an examining board, the state, or the work itself?

4Scope

Who might learn?

Everyone, a class or the qualified few, those allowed by birth, sex or community, or a design to widen the door?

5Who Paid

Who paid?

A patron or endowment, the state, a mission, the community, households, donors, or the pupils' own work?

6Fate

What became of it?

Built and kept, kept in form but converted in purpose, adopted and let go, refused or defeated, or still open?

How to read the atlas. Each entry gives the document's setting and argument, answers the six questions, meets its strongest objection, names the essays in the series that use it, and ends with a reading you can edit (Your Readings explains how to keep it). Start from an entry in the contents, from a question above, or from the Big Picture. The contents can be arranged by part, by date, by any of the six answers, or by essay. The Library holds every source in the series plan.
